# The voice target

One deck, written by hand, that the engine is trying to sound like. Everything
in `brand-voice.md` describes the voice. This shows it finished.

> **⚠ NEVER PUT THIS FILE IN A PROMPT.**
> It is a deck on this page's own subject, which makes it a template rather than
> an example. Measured on this engine's own output: every example set on the
> page's own ground was copied verbatim into a published deck within a week, and
> no off-subject example ever was. `writer.check_leak` counts runs of four words
> shared with the prompt and fails the draft. If this file were loaded, that gate
> would fire on every line of it — correctly.
>
> This is for the people editing the prompts. The prompts carry the RULES derived
> from it, never its words.

## The deck

Subject: attention residue. Sophie Leroy, *Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work?* (2009).
Moment: you have been home an hour and your coat is still on the sofa.

| # | Slide |
|---|---|
| 1 | You did not come home. You came to the address. |
| 2 | The coat is on the sofa. The sofa is now a coat rack. You are now a guest. |
| 3 | Sophie Leroy found that unfinished work keeps a piece of your attention. Not all of it. Just enough. |
| 4 | Enough that somebody asks about your day and you answer a different question. |
| 5 | Work gets an ending. Someone sends a last message and a door closes. |
| 6 | Your evening gets nothing. It just starts, unannounced, like a film you walked into halfway. |
| 7 | So announce it. "That's the day." Out loud, to nobody, while hanging up the coat. |
| 8 | It feels ridiculous for about four days. Then the hallway stops being a waiting room. |
| 9 | Send this to the one who is home, technically. |

## What the engine has to copy, and it is not the words

**Every slide turns.** Nine slides, nine things the reader did not have a moment
earlier. Slide 5 does not restate slide 4 in fresh words; it introduces the
asymmetry that slide 6 then pays off. This is the single difference between a
deck people finish and a deck people abandon on slide 3, and it is the rule the
engine had no way to express — every gate it owns is satisfied by a slide that
repeats the one before it in new vocabulary.

**The name appears twice in nine slides, not nine times.** Nowhere in the deck
above is a pattern named on every card. A reader who has learned the name on
slide 1 is being told nothing when it arrives again on slide 7.

**The finding is stated once, in plain words, and then dropped.** Slide 3 does
the citation's whole job. Slides 4 to 7 carry the idea and never the sentence.
The researcher's surname appears on the citation line and nowhere else.

**The object anchors the deck; it does not run it.** The coat is on slides 1, 2
and 7 — three of nine. It is doing work each time. A deck that prints its scene
noun on all nine slides reads as a machine gripping the only concrete word it
has, which is what the door/coat/sofa decks were.

**The advice is short enough to say.** "That's the day." Three words. Not a
filled-in template with a time and a room in it.

## The failure this replaces

The deck that prompted the rewrite, in full:

> Doorway pause. Your coat on the sofa, you at the door.
> **doorway pause explains why the outside stays in the hallway long after you
> have stood your ground.**
> I will put my coat away at 6:02pm in the hallway.

Every one of those lines passed all seventy-seven gates. The name is present,
the accent is present, the thread word is present, the character count is
inside the cap, the implementation intention is present in its exact mandated
shape. Nothing in the engine could tell that the second line means nothing,
because nothing in the engine was measuring meaning.
